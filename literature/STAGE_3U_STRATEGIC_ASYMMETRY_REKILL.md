# Stage 3U — Strategic-Asymmetry Prior-Art Re-Kill

Date: 2026-09-04

## Candidate headline under attack

A fixed-fleet transit operator reallocates direction-specific service frequency in response to retail-generated passenger demand. With exogenous background demand concentrated in one direction, downstream retail price best responses can have opposite slopes: one retailer treats the rival price as a strategic substitute while the other treats it as a strategic complement.

## Important prior-art threats

### Tombak (2006), Strategic Asymmetry

Tombak explicitly defines and studies games in which one firm regards the rival's strategic variable as a complement while the other regards it as a substitute. Therefore **the concept of strategic asymmetry is not new** and must not be claimed as such.

Required distinction: the contribution would have to be a transport-network microfoundation and comparative theorem showing when scarce direction-specific service reallocation endogenously creates this asymmetry in downstream retail pricing.

### Transport-network price interaction

Recent transport-network work on platform business models shows that prices of some network components may be strategic substitutes while other OD-service prices are strategic complements. Therefore **mixed complement/substitute price interaction in transport networks is not itself new**.

Required distinction: the candidate model concerns two downstream retailers, not transport operators pricing complementary journey components; the strategic asymmetry arises because retail demand reallocates a common fixed fleet across directions.

### Public-transport fare/frequency competition

The transit literature establishes frequency as a strategic variable and commonly obtains fare complementarity and frequency substitutability in operator competition. Endogenous frequency provision and the Mohring effect are mature topics.

Required distinction: the preferred model does not have competing transport operators choosing frequency. A third-party operator reallocates a fixed service resource in response to demand generated by an otherwise standard retail Bertrand game.

### Hotelling network externalities

Hotelling models with positive/negative network effects already alter price stability, differentiation, profitability and the choice between price and quantity competition. Toshimitsu (Japanese Economic Review, 2026) studies network connectivity in a Hotelling market and derives nonstandard Bertrand/Cournot profit rankings and endogenous strategic-variable choices.

Required distinction: a reduced-form network-effect representation is not enough. The Stage-4 model must show that the **directional fixed-fleet allocation constraint** produces a result that disappears both when background directional demand is removed and when the operator stops responding to retail shopping demand.

### Houde (AER, 2012)

Houde's empirical gasoline-retailing model represents consumers by commuting paths, making spatial differentiation depend on the road network and direction of traffic flows. Therefore **using traffic direction and network topology in retail spatial competition is not new**.

Required distinction: Houde treats the network as a demand-geography object; the Stage-3U candidate endogenizes the supply of transport frequency as a response to retail demand.

## Nested-benchmark kill logic

The preferred theorem must disappear in each benchmark:

- B0: fixed equal frequencies — standard quadratic Hotelling; both price BR slopes `1/2`.
- B1: demand-responsive frequencies with `M=0` — symmetric equilibrium implies `H_xx=0`; both price BR slopes `1/2`.
- B2: `M>0` but transit frequencies do not respond to shopping demand — directional access shifts demand but leaves the Hotelling demand slope affine; both price BR slopes `1/2`.
- Full model: `M>0` and frequencies respond to shopping demand — `H_xx` can be nonzero and opposite-signed BR slopes are possible.

## Verdict

`SURVIVES STAGE 3 AS CONDITIONAL GO`.

The route is eligible for a narrow Stage-4 kill test, but the paper cannot sell “strategic asymmetry” as a new concept. It must establish a new **institution-specific theorem**: endogenous scarce transit-frequency reallocation creates one-sided strategic substitutability in retail prices under directional background demand, with exact conditions and welfare consequences that cannot be reproduced by the minimum nested benchmarks.
