\begin{abstract}
We study spatial retail price competition when access quality is supplied by a third-party transport operator that reallocates a fixed service resource across directions in response to passenger demand. One direction also carries exogenous background travel demand. Retail prices therefore affect market shares directly and indirectly through service reallocation, which improves own access while worsening rival access. We show that a nonempty open set of global pure-strategy Hotelling--Bertrand equilibria exhibits one-sided strategic substitutability: one retailer lowers its price when the rival raises price, while the other retains an upward-sloping price response. A minimum service obligation can be strictly slack at equilibrium yet support the global equilibrium by truncating extreme off-equilibrium service collapse. Because the operator minimizes aggregate waiting cost, an envelope identity links the access term in shopper demand to marginal real waiting cost. The decentralized equilibrium then generally differs from the same-floor second-best allocation. The mechanism is locally robust to power waiting-cost specifications and is naturally interpreted in directional transit, shuttle, and demand-responsive fleet-allocation settings.
\end{abstract}
