\section{Related literature}\label{sec:literature}

The paper combines familiar ingredients, so the relevant comparison is not whether any one ingredient is new, but whether the complete strategic chain is already contained in an existing model.

The retail side is standard spatial price competition. We use the quadratic endpoint benchmark associated with \citet{hotelling1929} and \citet{daspremont1979}; retailer locations are fixed and no contribution is claimed for the transport-cost technology. The closest reduced-form threat instead comes from differentiated-price models with network or clientele effects. In that literature, market-share feedback can intensify competition, generate multiplicity, and produce asymmetric outcomes \citep{grilo2001,grivavettas2011,tolottiyepez2020,toshimitsu2026}. Our local retail demand system can likewise be written as a nonlinear share-dependent term. We therefore do not treat market-share feedback, multiplicity, or asymmetry as novel. Nor is the sign pattern itself new: \citet{tombak2006} formalizes strategic asymmetry, and transport-network pricing can contain complement and substitute forces simultaneously \citep{mchardy2024}.

A second comparison concerns vertical transport markets and scarce upstream resources. Transportation economics already studies how infrastructure charges, service quality, frequency, and capacity interact with downstream carrier pricing and competition \citep{bassozhang2007,depalma2018,alvarez2020}. More generally, upstream capacity-allocation rules can reshape downstream retailer competition \citep{chenli2013}. These precedents rule out any claim that an upstream transport layer or scarce resource affecting downstream prices is itself new. The present mechanism differs in the continuation: the upstream operator does not choose an access price or expand capacity, but reallocates one fixed shared service resource in response to downstream trip demand. A demand gain therefore changes generalized access costs on both sides of the downstream market.

A third comparison is transit service design. The demand--frequency feedback is a classic scale-economy mechanism \citep{mohring1972}, and vicious or virtuous cycles with multiple equilibria have been analyzed directly \citep{baryosef2013}. Minimum service requirements, fixed-fleet constraints, and directional service reallocation are likewise familiar operations primitives. Deadheading and short-turning can concentrate effective service when directional demand is imbalanced \citep{furth1985,cortes2011}, while demand-responsive systems make finite-fleet allocation and repositioning across zones explicit \citep{liuouyang2021}. The paper uses these ideas as an institutional microfoundation rather than as separate novelty claims.

What remains is the interaction across the three layers:
\begin{equation*}
\text{retail price}
\rightarrow \text{shopping demand}
\rightarrow \text{third-party shared-service reallocation}
\rightarrow \text{own and rival access}
\rightarrow \text{shopping demand}
\rightarrow \text{retail best responses}.
\end{equation*}
Directional background demand makes this chain asymmetric, while a minimum service floor can remain slack on path and alter only large off-path reallocations. The prior-art audit did not identify a single predecessor that combines this demand-responsive third-party continuation with a global Hotelling--Bertrand equilibrium exhibiting one-sided strategic substitutability. We therefore position the paper as a narrow transport/spatial-competition contribution, not as a general theorem about network externalities, vertical markets, or capacity allocation.
