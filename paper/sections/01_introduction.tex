\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Access to a commercial destination is often treated as an exogenous characteristic of spatial competition. In many transport systems, however, service quality responds to passenger demand. A destination that attracts more trips may receive more frequent service, more vehicles, or more intensive fleet positioning. When the operator has a fixed service resource, that gain can come at the expense of another direction or zone. Retail pricing can then change not only who shops where, but also the transport service through which consumers reach competing destinations.

This paper studies that feedback in a simple Hotelling--Bertrand model. Two retail destinations compete in prices at the endpoints of a linear market. A third-party transport operator allocates a fixed total service resource across the two directions in response to total passenger demand. One direction also carries exogenous background demand, such as commuting or education trips. A retailer's price cut therefore raises its shopping demand, induces the operator to shift service toward that direction, and withdraws service from the rival direction. The resulting access response feeds back into shopping demand. Because the background demand is directional, the feedback need not affect the two retailers symmetrically.

The first result is that this transport-mediated feedback can reverse the strategic relation for only one retailer. There exists a nonempty open set of primitives for which the model has a global pure-strategy price Nash equilibrium satisfying
\begin{equation*}
\frac{dBR_L}{dp_R}<0<\frac{dBR_R}{dp_L}.
\end{equation*}
Thus one retailer lowers its price when the rival raises price, while the other retains the conventional upward-sloping response. Strategic asymmetry itself is a known game-theoretic phenomenon \citep{tombak2006}, and mixed strategic relations also arise in transport-network pricing \citep{mchardy2024}. The contribution is narrower: the pattern arises here from a third-party operator reallocating a shared physical service resource in response to downstream demand, and survives the global retail-price game rather than appearing only in local first-order conditions.

The second result concerns the role of a minimum service obligation. Without a lower service bound, a sufficiently large price-induced demand shift can drive service in the losing direction toward zero and create multiple fulfilled-expectations continuations. This type of demand--frequency feedback is familiar in transit economics \citep{baryosef2013}. We impose a minimum service share, a standard transport-service primitive, and show that it can be strictly slack at the equilibrium yet essential for global equilibrium support. It changes only sufficiently large off-equilibrium reallocations. For the exact existence witness, the same on-path equilibrium is supported over an intermediate range of nonbinding floor levels: a weaker floor permits the original tipping deviation, while a stronger floor creates a profitable deviation for the protected side. We do not interpret this range as an optimal regulatory rule.

A further welfare implication follows from the transport microfoundation. Conditional on retail shares, the operator minimizes aggregate waiting cost. The envelope theorem therefore implies that the directional waiting-cost difference entering the marginal shopper's choice is exactly the derivative of minimized aggregate waiting cost. Retail prices cancel as transfers in welfare, and the decentralized price equilibrium generally does not minimize real spatial and waiting costs. At the exact global-equilibrium witness, the retail equilibrium assigns $57.5\%$ of shoppers to the high-background-demand side, while the constrained efficient allocation subject to the same service floor assigns approximately $70.24\%$. The resulting real-cost reduction is about $1.22\%$ under the witness normalization. We use this welfare mapping to interpret the transport mechanism; we do not claim that envelope arguments or private--social allocation wedges are new in general.

The mechanism is identified by nested benchmarks. If service is fixed, if directional background demand is removed, or if service does not respond to shopping demand, the opposite-signed local price reactions disappear. The result is also locally robust when waiting disutility is generalized from $w/f$ to $w f^{-\rho}$ around $\rho=1$. The exact witness is used only to establish existence and global optimality; the economic claim is the open-set mechanism, not the particular parameter vector.

The model is intended for settings in which downstream destinations are large enough to affect discretionary trip demand and service allocation. Directional shuttle or fixed-route operations using deadheading or short-turning provide one interpretation \citep{furth1985,cortes2011}; demand-responsive or shared-mobility fleets repositioned across zones provide another \citep{liuouyang2021}. The operator is a service-planning layer with a fixed resource, not a private platform whose fare, fleet size, or network design is modeled. We do not claim that an ordinary small retailer can move transit frequency, nor do we calibrate the model to a particular city pair.

The paper is related to the literatures on spatial competition, network externalities, vertical transport markets, capacity allocation, and transit service design. Network effects in Hotelling price competition are well established \citep{grilo2001,grivavettas2011,tolottiyepez2020,toshimitsu2026}; upstream transport facilities and service quality have long been studied jointly with downstream carrier competition \citep{bassozhang2007,depalma2018,alvarez2020}; and scarce upstream capacity allocation is known to affect downstream competition \citep{chenli2013}. Accordingly, the paper's claim is deliberately narrower: the interaction of downstream price competition with a demand-responsive third-party operator that reallocates one common fixed service resource across asymmetric directions. Section~\ref{sec:literature} develops this distinction in detail.
