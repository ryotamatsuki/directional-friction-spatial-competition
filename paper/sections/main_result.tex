\section{Directional friction and the price effect of separation}\label{sec:result}

The price response to greater separation follows directly from \eqref{eq:pLstar}--\eqref{eq:pRstar}:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial p_L^*}{\partial r}
=\frac{2r+2+\theta}{3}>0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial p_R^*}{\partial r}
=\frac{4-2r-\theta}{3}.
\label{eq:price_derivatives}
\end{equation}
Thus the left firm's equilibrium price always rises when firm $R$ is moved farther to the right, whereas the right firm's own price falls whenever $\theta>4-2r$. The remaining question is whether this reversal can occur inside the strict global-equilibrium region of Proposition~\ref{prop:global}.

Define
\begin{equation}
\bar r=\frac{\sqrt{17}-1}{4}.
\end{equation}
The lower bound required for the price reversal and the upper bound required for the sufficient global-equilibrium condition overlap if and only if
\begin{equation}
4-2r<\frac{2r(5r-2)}{2-r},
\end{equation}
which is equivalent to $2r^2+r-2>0$, or $r>\bar r$.

\begin{corollary}[Opposite price responses to greater separation]\label{cor:reversal}
For
\begin{equation}
r\in(\bar r,1),
\qquad
4-2r<\theta<\frac{2r(5r-2)}{2-r},
\label{eq:reversalregion}
\end{equation}
the strict global price equilibrium in Proposition~\ref{prop:global} satisfies
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{\partial p_L^*}{\partial r}>0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial p_R^*}{\partial r}<0.}
\label{eq:reversal}
\end{equation}
The set in \eqref{eq:reversalregion} is open and nonempty.
\end{corollary}

Corollary~\ref{cor:reversal} isolates the paper's main point. A larger $r$ has the familiar competition-softening effect: greater horizontal separation weakens direct price competition. But the same movement also increases the one-sided access burden faced by marginal consumers who buy from firm $R$. For firm $L$, greater separation continues to support a higher equilibrium price. For firm $R$, however, the additional directional exposure can dominate competition softening. In that case, moving the firm farther away lowers its own equilibrium mill price even while its rival raises its price.

The standard quadratic Hotelling benchmark makes the contrast transparent. Setting $\theta=0$ gives
\begin{equation}
p_L^*-c=\frac{r(r+2)}{3},
\qquad
p_R^*-c=\frac{r(4-r)}{3},
\end{equation}
so
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial p_L^*}{\partial r}=\frac{2r+2}{3}>0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial p_R^*}{\partial r}=\frac{4-2r}{3}>0.
\end{equation}
Hence the opposite-sign response is generated by the directional component rather than by quadratic differentiation alone.

A concrete point inside the reversal region is $r=4/5$ and $\theta=5/2$. At this point
\begin{equation}
x^*=\frac{337}{615},\quad
p_L^*-c=\frac{337}{150},\quad
p_R^*-c=\frac{139}{75},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial p_L^*}{\partial r}=\frac{61}{30}>0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial p_R^*}{\partial r}=-\frac{1}{30}<0.
\end{equation}
The example is not needed for the proof, but it illustrates that the reversal is not a knife-edge equality.
