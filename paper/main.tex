\title{Directional Access Friction and the Price Effect of Spatial Separation}
\author{}

\begin{abstract}
This paper studies how directional access friction changes the price effect of spatial separation in a Hotelling duopoly. Consumers are uniformly distributed on a line, firms charge uniform mill prices, and access cost combines quadratic distance with an additional one-sided cost incurred when consumers move toward the right-hand firm. Holding locations fixed isolates the pricing consequence of a change in separation. We derive a strict global pure-strategy price equilibrium on an explicit open parameter region. In the standard quadratic benchmark, moving the right-hand firm farther from its rival raises both firms' equilibrium prices. With sufficiently strong directional friction, the same displacement instead raises the left firm's price while lowering the moving right firm's own price. Greater separation therefore has two opposing effects for the directionally exposed firm: it softens price competition but also increases the access burden borne by marginal consumers who buy from that firm. When the latter effect dominates, the usual competition-softening price response is reversed. The result is deliberately narrow: it concerns the price subgame and does not characterize endogenous locations.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Hotelling competition; spatial pricing; directional access friction; horizontal differentiation; asymmetric transport costs.\\
