# Stage 5U — Minimum-Frequency / Demand-Frequency Prior-Art Check

Date: 2026-09-04

## Purpose

Stage 4 authorized one repair only: a minimum public-service frequency in each direction. This mini-audit checks whether that ingredient has an independent institutional/theoretical rationale and identifies the new literature threats it creates.

## Finding 1 — minimum frequency is a standard service/fleet constraint

Minimum frequency/headway constraints are standard in transit network design and frequency-setting problems and may be imposed to guarantee minimum service. They are commonly combined with fleet-size constraints. Therefore

\[
f_i\ge \underline f
\]

is not introduced solely to manufacture the desired retail-pricing result.

Relevant examples / institutional sources checked:

- U.S. Federal Transit Administration guidance describes headway/frequency standards and notes that a standard may establish a minimum service frequency by area.
- Transit network design/frequency-setting models impose minimum frequency together with fleet constraints; a 2026 Networks and Spatial Economics model explicitly includes both.
- Frequency-setting models in public transport commonly bound frequencies below and above; the lower bound represents minimum service requirements.
- Herbon & Hadas (2015), *Determining optimal frequency and vehicle capacity for public transit routes*, uses regulator-imposed service constraints to assure a minimal transit service level.

## Finding 2 — demand-frequency vicious/virtuous cycles are prior art

Bar-Yosef, Martens & Benenson (2013), *A model of the vicious cycle of a bus line*, Transportation Research Part B 54, 37–50, directly models the feedback

`demand -> frequency/service quality -> demand`

and obtains multiple equilibria / vicious and virtuous cycles.

Accordingly, the Stage-4 tipping result is not itself new and Stage 5 must not sell the minimum service floor as a novel cure for a newly discovered transit phenomenon.

The classic Mohring effect is likewise prior art: higher patronage can justify higher frequency, reduce waiting time, and further increase effective service attractiveness.

## Finding 3 — the remaining novelty target is downstream competition

The Stage-5 claim, if it survives Stage 6, must be narrower:

> a directionally asymmetric, demand-responsive allocation of a scarce fixed transit fleet can create one-sided strategic substitutability in downstream Hotelling-Bertrand retail prices; a standard minimum-service obligation can eliminate the off-equilibrium transit tipping that otherwise destroys the global retail-price equilibrium while remaining slack at equilibrium.

The exact novelty burden is therefore not the existence of frequency floors, Mohring feedback, multiple transit equilibria, or strategic asymmetry separately. It is the proposition-level interaction between those transport-supply features and downstream spatial price competition.

## Stage-6 threats carried forward

1. **Bar-Yosef–Martens–Benenson (2013):** absorbs the demand-frequency multiple-equilibrium/vicious-cycle mechanism.
2. **Transit frequency regulation literature:** absorbs minimum frequency as an institutional/theoretical primitive.
3. **Tombak (2006):** absorbs strategic asymmetry as a general concept.
4. **Hotelling/network-externality models:** may absorb the retail price-curvature result after the operator is reduced out.
5. **Transport-network pricing models with mixed strategic relations:** may absorb the complement/substitute sign pattern at a broader network level.

## Mini-audit verdict

`INGREDIENT JUSTIFIED; NOVELTY NOT ESTABLISHED`.

The minimum-service floor passes Stage 5's microfoundation/institutional-rationale requirement, but it adds no independent novelty credit. All surviving novelty must be re-killed at the proposition level in Stage 6.
