\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

A standard intuition in spatial competition is that greater separation softens price competition. In quadratic Hotelling models, moving products farther apart tends to relax direct competitive pressure and support higher equilibrium prices; this logic underlies the familiar stability result of \citet{DAspremontGabszewiczThisse1979}. The intuition is symmetric: greater distance makes the two products less substitutable in the same qualitative way for both firms.

Many spatial environments, however, are not directionally neutral. Reaching a destination on one side of a market can impose an additional access burden that is absent in the opposite direction. Examples include one-way circulation, directional schedule or travel frictions, and other spatial layouts in which moving toward one side is systematically more costly. The question in this note is narrow: how does such one-sided access friction change the equilibrium price effect of greater spatial separation?

I study a covered Hotelling duopoly with quadratic distance costs and an additional soft directional term. The left firm is fixed at zero and the right firm at $r$. Locations are exogenous so that the analysis isolates the pricing subgame. The directional term applies only when a consumer travels rightward to a destination. I solve the price game globally rather than relying only on local first-order conditions, which is important because price deviations can move the marginal consumer across a kink in the transport-cost schedule.

The main result is an opposite price response to the same increase in separation. On a nonempty open parameter region, moving the right, directionally exposed firm farther from its fixed rival raises the rival's equilibrium mill price but lowers the moving firm's own price. The mechanism is a conflict between two effects. Greater separation has the usual competition-softening effect, but it also increases the directional access burden borne by marginal consumers who buy from the right firm. When directional exposure dominates for that firm, its equilibrium price falls even though its rival's price continues to rise. In the standard quadratic benchmark obtained by setting the directional term to zero, both price derivatives are positive.

The contribution is intentionally limited. Prior work already shows that asymmetric transportation costs matter for spatial competition \citep{Nilssen1997,VanderWeijdeVerhoefVandenBerg2014}, that Hotelling prices can respond perversely to differentiation \citep{MerelSexton2010}, and that other forms of asymmetry can move firms' prices in opposite directions \citep{CohenHeifetz2024}. Recent work also develops related appeal-versus-competition mechanisms in horizontal differentiation \citep{ChakravortySappington2026}. The result here isolates a different comparative static: the same one-firm spatial displacement generates a rival-price increase and an own-price decrease because of one-sided directional exposure. Relative to the endogenous-location analysis of \citet{Ago2023}, the present note is narrower and treats locations as given, but provides an exact global price-equilibrium region for this directional price reversal.

The remainder of the paper defines the model, establishes the strict global price equilibrium, derives the reversal condition and its standard Hotelling benchmark, and then discusses the relation to the spatial-competition literature.
