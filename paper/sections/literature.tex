\section{Discussion and relation to the literature}\label{sec:literature}

The result is deliberately narrower than the general literatures on asymmetric transportation costs and nonstandard price effects in Hotelling models. Directional or asymmetric transport costs have long been known to alter spatial incentives. \citet{Nilssen1997}, for example, studies sequential location when transportation costs differ by direction, while \citet{VanderWeijdeVerhoefVandenBerg2014} combine asymmetric transport or schedule-delay costs with price-sensitive demand and spatial timing/location choices. Hard one-sided restrictions have also been studied in the unidirectional Hotelling line; \citet{Kharbach2009} proposes such a location-price model, and \citet{EbinaShimizu2012} revisits its equilibrium characterization. The present paper does not claim novelty for directionality itself. Its object is the price response to the same marginal displacement of one firm under a soft, one-sided access cost.

Nor is a falling price under greater differentiation novel in general. \citet{MerelSexton2010} show that Hotelling prices can display perverse comparative statics in weak-duopoly regimes. Likewise, opposite movements in two firms' prices can arise from other forms of asymmetry. In a quadratic Hotelling setting with quality differences, \citet{CohenHeifetz2024} obtain asymmetric pricing responses associated with vertical differentiation. These results rule out broad claims such as ``greater differentiation can lower price'' or ``asymmetry can make prices move in opposite directions'' as contributions of the present model.

The closest recent mechanism-level comparison is \citet{ChakravortySappington2026}, where consumers can incur a cost to alter a default horizontal characteristic. Their analysis highlights a trade-off between a product's appeal to consumers and the intensity with which the seller competes for them. The directional-exposure channel here is related in spirit, but the comparative-static object is different: the present result changes one firm's spatial location while holding the rival fixed and identifies an open region in which that same movement raises the rival's price and lowers the moving firm's own price.

For the journal-specific spatial-theory benchmark, \citet{Ago2023} shows that a small extension of Hotelling utility can support a range of endogenous symmetric locations and generate nonmonotone price and profit responses. Ago's contribution is broader as a location-theory result because firms' locations are endogenous. The present analysis intentionally does not solve a location game. Instead, it isolates a globally valid pricing implication of exogenous spatial separation under directional access friction. What is added is therefore not another claim that a modified utility function changes Hotelling outcomes, but a specific directional mechanism: increasing the right firm's separation simultaneously softens price competition and raises the directional burden borne by consumers buying from that firm. The second effect can reverse only the moving firm's usual price response.

This scope also identifies the main limitation. Corollary~\ref{cor:reversal} is established for the directional cost in \eqref{eq:tau}; no claim is made that the same sign pattern holds for arbitrary asymmetric transport technologies. The value of the result is instead its exact characterization within a transparent spatial model and the fact that it survives global price deviations rather than appearing only as a local first-order-condition effect.
