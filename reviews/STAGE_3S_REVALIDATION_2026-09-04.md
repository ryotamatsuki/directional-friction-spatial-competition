The candidate primitive

## Revalidation of E — price-effect reversal

On the middle-sharing local branch, the clean result remains

\[
\frac{\partial p_L}{\partial d}>0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial p_R}{\partial d}=\frac{4-2r-\theta}{3}.
\]

Hence larger separation can lower the right firm's candidate price for sufficiently high directional friction.

This is a genuine algebraic effect, but the Stage-3 standard requires more than a conditional sign comparative static. The closest asymmetric-distance-cost literature already allows side-specific price and location responses. A full global-price proof would therefore require substantial work for a result whose novelty ceiling remains low.

**Revalidated decision: DO NOT PROMOTE.**

## Revalidation of H — exposure versus differentiation

The right-side follower local stationary root remains

\[
b_+(a,\theta)=\frac{2a+2}{3}-\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac16\sqrt{4(a-2)^2-3\theta^2},
\]

with

\[
\frac{\partial b_+}{\partial\theta}<0
\]

wherever real and locally valid.

The channel is economically meaningful: moving farther from the rival softens price competition but also increases directional exposure. Nevertheless, asymmetric/interior differentiation under asymmetric transport costs is already a crowded result class, especially given Nilssen (1997), Sun (2014), and van der Weijde, Verhoef & van den Berg (2014).

**Revalidated decision: DO NOT PROMOTE.**

## Revalidation of B — side switching

A price-mediated switch in the follower's preferred side would be stronger than a local comparative static. However, proving it requires complete price continuation for both orderings and a global location comparison. The closest prior literature already covers entry patterns and positional advantages under asymmetric/directional travel costs. The expected novelty gain remains too small relative to proof burden.

**Revalidated decision: DO NOT PROMOTE.**

## Post-Stage-3S evidence: Stage 3T closes the E×H uplift route

The subsequent Stage 3T branch provides stronger negative evidence than was available at the original Stage 3S gate.

Three interaction hypotheses were tested:

1. endogenous location crosses the price-reversal frontier;
2. the price reversal causes the endpoint/interior location transition;
3. the right firm optimally differentiates farther even while its own price falls with differentiation.

All three were killed.

The price-reversal frontier is

\[
r_F(\theta)=\frac{4-\theta}{2},
\]

while the positive interior stationary location is

\[
r_+(\theta)=\frac{4-3\theta+\sqrt{16-3\theta^2}}{6}.
\]

Their gap is bounded away from zero on the tested branch, so endogenous location does not cross the price-reversal frontier. The endpoint/interior location switch occurs while differentiation still raises the right firm's own equilibrium price. Finally, in the price-reversal region both right-firm price and demand fall with additional separation, so the right firm cannot optimally move farther into that region on the validated branch.

This eliminates the strongest attempt to turn E plus H into a full-game-only theorem.

## Additional candidate K — directional access-improvement policy

Because the motivating application is urban-to-peripheral accessibility, Stage 3S revalidation also tests a policy-centered rescue:

> a government reduces the one-sided friction `theta`; firms then relocate and reprice; access improvement may increase peripheral market share while reducing peripheral profit and changing total welfare.

This is institutionally attractive but not a defensible rescue route.

The infrastructure/spatial-competition literature already contains closely related chains. Combes & Linnemer (2000), in a Hotelling-type model with discriminatory pricing, study creation of new transport infrastructure, solve firm location and price equilibrium, obtain relocation, lower firm profits and regional welfare asymmetries, and study optimal infrastructure location/pricing. Schram & Sonnemans-type political-infrastructure spatial-competition work and later transport-infrastructure literature also explicitly treat infrastructure as a determinant of product-market competition and long-run location.

The proposed policy route therefore risks becoming another parameterization of the established mechanism:

\[
\text{infrastructure} \rightarrow \text{market access/competition} \rightarrow \text{firm relocation/prices} \rightarrow \text{welfare}.
\]

The directional primitive could change signs or thresholds, but without a new private-market theorem first this does not satisfy the workflow novelty standard.

**Candidate K decision: KILL AS STANDALONE RESCUE.**

## September 2026 recent-literature check

Targeted searches for 2024–2026 work did not identify an exact paper using `theta(y-x)_+` in the same two-stage model. This is not evidence of novelty.

Recent work reinforces the broader crowding of Hotelling extensions: 2025 research studies quantity-dependent transport costs and elastic demand; 2026 work studies additional equilibrium multiplicity under quadratic Hotelling with incomplete coverage. Neither rescues the directional route, but both raise the standard for another functional-form extension.

## Closest prior-art threats after revalidation

- Nilssen (1997): asymmetric directional transportation costs, sequential location, entry pattern, first-mover advantage.
- Lai (2001): sequential directional markets and continuous-space nonexistence.
- Colombo (2011) and related unidirectional Hotelling work: two-stage location-price competition under hard directionality.
- Sun (2014): directional delivery constraints with agglomeration, maximum differentiation and asymmetric dispersion.
- van der Weijde, Verhoef & van den Berg (2014): asymmetric distance/schedule-delay costs with endogenous locations and fares, alternative timing, Stackelberg structures and regulation.
- Cohen & Heifetz (2024): asymmetric location-price competition and a nontrivial fixed-differentiation result.
- Hamoudi & Moral / Hamoudi & Martín-Bustamante: nonlinear transport costs, global price deviations and location-pair price-equilibrium regions.
- Combes & Linnemer (2000): transport infrastructure, endogenous firm relocation/pricing, firm-profit effects, regional welfare and optimal infrastructure.

## Whole-game novelty assessment

After revalidation, every plausible route falls into one of the following categories:

1. **structurally absorbed:** mover advantage, asymmetric location, infrastructure-induced relocation, existence-region effects;
2. **result-class crowded:** interior/maximal/agglomerated differentiation or side ordering;
3. **too narrow:** local sign reversal of a price derivative;
4. **mechanically embedded:** effective-center/captive-market shifts directly implied by the one-sided generalized-cost term;
5. **failed interaction:** E×H does not generate the hoped-for full-game theorem on the validated branch.

No candidate satisfies the Stage-3 success criterion of a clear new strategic feedback plus a proposition-level result not obtainable as an immediate corollary or close analogue of the benchmark literatures.
