\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studies spatial retail price competition when access is not fixed but is supplied by a third-party operator reallocating a scarce service resource. A retail price change then affects demand twice: directly through the shopper's price comparison and indirectly through the operator's service response. Directional background demand makes this feedback asymmetric, and a shared fleet makes improved access to one direction costly to the rival direction.

The model delivers three main conclusions. First, a nonempty open set of global Hotelling--Bertrand equilibria exhibits one-sided strategic substitutability: one retailer lowers its price when the rival raises price while the other retains the conventional complementary response. Second, a minimum service obligation can be irrelevant on the equilibrium path yet essential for global equilibrium support because it rules out extreme off-path service collapse. Third, the operator's cost-minimization problem yields an envelope identity that links the access term in shopper demand to marginal real waiting cost, making the private--social allocation wedge transparent.

The results should be interpreted narrowly. Strategic asymmetry, network effects in spatial competition, demand--frequency feedback, and minimum transit-service standards are all established ideas. The contribution is their particular full-game interaction through a third-party fixed-resource operator. The paper also does not derive an optimal service standard, transit subsidy, fleet size, or retailer location. Those decisions would change the strategic environment and lie outside the frozen model.

The most natural applications are large destination zones, commercial clusters, directional shuttle or short-turn operations, and demand-responsive or shared-mobility systems in which a finite fleet is repositioned across zones. Empirical work could test whether destination-demand shocks generate the predicted cross-direction service response and whether the resulting pricing reactions differ across high- and low-background-demand destinations. Such evidence would complement, rather than alter, the theoretical mechanism studied here.
